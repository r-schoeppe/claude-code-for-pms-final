Welcome. This is the story of one release, 4.2, and what I found in my first six modules as the new product manager for Rook Dispatch. It shipped with no measure of success, and nobody could tell it was going wrong. Four of our sixteen responders almost stopped being asked to take jobs. In about fifteen minutes: what happened, how I found it, the fix, and how we make sure it does not happen again.
The short version

Four answers, before the details.

1What happened

After release 4.2, four responders went from 49 job offers a week to 3.

2Most likely why

A missed offer costs as much as a refusal, and a score never recovers on its own. The engineer who built it still has to confirm.

3The fix

Let a missed offer fade after a week, and show people where they stand.

4Never again

A skill checks every brief for an owner, a decision, a measure, one scope, and the problem first.

Five words you need

A job offer is called a ping.

Callout an incident that needs someonePing an offer on one responder's phoneResponder the person who goesHandler looks after a responder, uses the web consoleScore how often someone has said yes lately. Higher means asked sooner
1 · Callout comes in"A woman in a beekeeping suit is emptying a bank."
2 · RankBest match first, by distance, skills and the score.
3 · Ping the top oneThey have 60 seconds to answer.
4 · Three outcomestakenturned downmissed
5 · Next in lineAfter a refusal or a miss, the next responder is pinged.
Quick vocabulary so everyone can follow. A callout is an incident. A ping is the offer of that incident to one responder's phone, one person at a time. They have sixty seconds. They take it, turn it down, or miss it. Who gets asked first depends on distance, skills, and a score for how often they have said yes lately. Keep the score in mind: it is the whole story.

Why it happened: the rule inside the code

A miss costs more than a yes earns.

0.50
a responder's score, starts at 0.50, lowest possible 0.00

Try it: miss three pings in a row, then try to climb back.

0.12 a miss or a refusal0.08 a yesnever fades on its own
This is the actual rule from the code. Take a ping and the score goes up by eight hundredths. Miss one or turn one down and it goes down by twelve. Try it: miss three in a row and see how many yeses it takes to get back. The score never fades with time. The only way up is to be asked again and say yes. Hold on to this rule. In module four I test it against the real pings.

What I learned

What changed in how I work.

Took the handover at its word→Tested it. "Mostly seasonal" was half right: callouts dipped 12%, but misses and the four were not seasonal.
Trusted the average→Looked inside it. One acceptance rate hid four people.
Asked what the code does→Asked what it does not do: no recovery, no delivery check.
Built the prototype first→Brief first, then prototype, then a review by the people who would use it.
These are the four habits that changed. I stopped taking the handover at its word and tested it. I stopped trusting the average, because it hid four people. I started asking what the code does not do, which is where the real gap was. And I now write the brief before the prototype. Those habits are what the two skills I built try to make automatic.
The fix

Let a miss fade, and tell people it has.

Today

One bad run is permanent. The four score 0.00, the lowest possible, and are not asked.

The fix is deliberately small. A miss still costs the same, but the penalty halves every two days, so after about a week it is gone. How we will know it worked

From 1.8% of all offers to at least 14%.

The measure

In the second full week after launch, the four get at least 14% of all weekly offers. Today it is 1.8%. Before 4.2 it was 28%.

We undo the fix if, over a rolling week, any one of these happens
Fewer than 66.7% of offers are taken
More than 12.9% of callouts go untaken
The slowest 10% of callouts take over 82 seconds to reach a responder
We check daily. Each line is a level reached in the weeks right after 4.2, so going past it means we are worse than the damage we are fixing.
How it reaches 14%: a healed score puts them back within about 5 minutes of travel behind a healthy neighbour, so they are asked when close. That is a replay, not a forecast; 14% is our proposal. What it will not fix: why the four miss 70% of their first offers. It may be the phone not receiving the offer, or slow answers. The shorter wait is the likely trigger for the extra misses; whether to keep 60 seconds is a separate decision, and nobody owns it yet.
How will we know it worked? The four responders should get at least fourteen percent of all weekly offers, up from under two. Before the release it was twenty-eight. And we have written down in advance when we would undo it: any of three tripwires. One limit to be straight about: this does not explain why those four miss so many first offers, and the sixty second wait is the likely trigger. That is a separate decision with no owner yet.
